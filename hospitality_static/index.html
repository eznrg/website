Why hotels are different

A hotel is a distinct energy system.

Lodging is 3% of U.S. commercial buildings but 9% of fuels consumption — and water heating and space heating alone account for almost 40% of that usage. The load behaves unlike any other commercial property.

Energy is a product input

Your property converts energy into a continuous guest experience — conditioned rooms, hot water, laundry, food service, amenity availability. That makes energy a cost center and an input to the product at the same time.

Occupancy alone doesn't explain it

Load is driven by occupancy, weather, and service intensity together. Two nights with the same room count can produce very different energy outcomes depending on temperature, events, and laundry timing.

The monthly bill hides the answer

A bill shows how much you consumed — not when you created cost, where you had flexibility, or which subsystem drove the deviation. Interval data, weather, and occupancy context are what make those questions answerable.

The load shape is the asset

Your peaks don't line up with the grid's.

Across four metered ComEd-territory properties, every meter set its annual peak in January — and every peak landed in a morning or evening ramp hour. None peaked on a summer afternoon.

PJM's system and capacity peaks fall on hot summer afternoons. These properties run near their seasonal minimum in exactly those hours — and hit their own peaks when the wider grid is calm.

That inversion is an advantage. Billing-month peak demand runs 127 kW in January but collapses to 46–47 kW across June, July, and August. Because delivery charges are set by each month's highest kW interval, the heaviest demand-charge burden lands in winter, not summer.

A load that spikes far above its own baseline for a handful of ramp hours is, by definition, a load with a large controllable slice — the raw material for demand-charge management, demand response, and storage dispatch.

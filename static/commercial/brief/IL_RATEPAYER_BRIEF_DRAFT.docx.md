

		

## **Executive summary**

Every month, millions of Illinois residents and businesses open an electric bill, glance at the total, and move on. That is understandable. A hotel owner has guests to serve. A manufacturer has production to run. A property manager has tenants to answer. A homeowner has a life. But behind that one total is a surprisingly sophisticated system: wholesale markets, state procurement rules, utility delivery tariffs, monthly adjustment mechanisms, supplier contracts, capacity obligations, taxes, and riders. When I first entered the energy industry, I assumed electricity pricing was straightforward. It is not. 

The deeper I dug, the clearer the real issue became: many customers are not ignoring the details because they are careless. 

They are ignoring them because no one has explained the system in plain English. 

---

The purpose of this brief is not to argue that ComEd default service is always better, or that retail supply is always worse. It is to explain how the system works so Illinois ratepayers can compare options with a better benchmark.

# **Analysis Fundamentals**

A short note, then the numbers.

## **What is measured**

In northern Illinois, ComEd is the regulated delivery utility. ComEd owns and operates the local distribution system that brings electricity to homes and businesses. If a storm knocks out service, a meter issue occurs, or local wires need repair, the customer still contacts ComEd. Choosing a third-party supplier does not change the physical delivery path. The supply side is different. Illinois allows customers in the ComEd, Ameren Illinois, and MidAmerican service territories to choose who supplies the electricity supply portion of service: the public utility default supply option or an Alternative Retail Electric Supplier, commonly called an ARES. PJM sits upstream from the customer bill. It is the regional grid operator and wholesale market administrator for a large multistate region that includes the ComEd territory. PJM does not own the power plants or power lines it coordinates. It balances supply and demand, monitors reliability, and runs wholesale markets for energy, capacity, and ancillary services.

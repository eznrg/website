Illinois commercial energy

The headline rate is not the benchmark.

Every month, Illinois businesses open an electric bill, glance at the total, and move on. That is understandable — behind that one number sit wholesale markets, state procurement rules, delivery tariffs, monthly adjustments, supplier contracts, and riders. Most customers are not ignoring the details because they are careless. They are ignoring them because no one has explained the system in plain English.

What is measured

Three things the total doesn't spell out.

Illinois lets customers in the ComEd, Ameren Illinois, and MidAmerican territories choose who supplies their electricity. Choosing does not change everything on the bill — and knowing which part it changes is most of the work.

ComEd delivers either way

ComEd owns and operates the local distribution system. If a storm knocks out service, a meter fails, or local wires need repair, you still call ComEd. Choosing a third-party supplier does not change the physical delivery path, and it does not change the delivery charges.

PJM sits upstream of the bill

PJM is the regional grid operator and wholesale market administrator for the territory. It owns none of the plants or lines it coordinates — it balances supply and demand, monitors reliability, and runs the wholesale markets for energy, capacity, and ancillary services that shape what suppliers must charge.

Only some line items move

Switching to a retail supplier replaces the supply charges and removes the monthly Purchased Electricity Adjustment. Delivery stays with ComEd. Taxes and fees stay put. A quoted rate that doesn't say which lines it actually replaces is not comparable to anything.

The benchmark moved

A fixed rate is only cheap relative to something.

The benchmark for default service is NET — the published Price to Compare plus that month's Purchased Electricity Adjustment. It is not a fixed number, and over the last thirty months it has not been a stable one either.

Through 2024 the default benchmark averaged 6.534¢ and rose above 7.8¢ in exactly one month — so a customer on a 7.8¢ fixed contract paid more than default service in the other eleven. In May 2025 the lines crossed again, and default service has stayed above the contract every month since.

The 2026 numbers are a different regime again: the default benchmark has averaged 11.614¢ year to date, with a single month at 17.870¢ — a month in which the adjustment alone added 8.166¢ on top of a 9.704¢ Price to Compare.

Over the two years the brief covers, the fixed contract lost money. 2025 recovered a good part of 2024's gap, but not all of it. That is the honest result, and it is the reason the useful number is not any single quoted rate but the break-even rate: across those 24 months, a fixed contract at 7.439¢ would have exactly matched default service. Below that you were ahead; above it you were behind.

Beyond the brief · EZ NRG

The brief's scenario tables stop at the end of 2025. Running the same method against the 2026 rates in its own appendix: six months of default service cost $20,904.90 against $14,040.00 on the 7.8¢ contract — $6,864.90 ahead in half a year, more than double what the contract gave up across the previous two combined.

That is not a claim that fixed contracts win. It is the same arithmetic pointed at more recent data, and it is why the break-even benchmark matters more than the quote: the rate that looked expensive in 2024 is the rate that is winning now.

01

Executive Summary

Every month, millions of Illinois residents and businesses open an electric bill, glance at the total, and move on. That is understandable. A hotel owner has guests to serve. A manufacturer has production to run. A property manager has tenants to answer. A homeowner has a life. But behind that one total is a surprisingly sophisticated system: wholesale markets, state procurement rules, utility delivery tariffs, monthly adjustment mechanisms, supplier contracts, capacity obligations, taxes, and riders. When I first entered the energy industry, I assumed electricity pricing was straightforward. It is not.

The deeper I dug, the clearer the real issue became: many customers are not ignoring the details because they are careless.

They are ignoring them because no one has explained the system in plain English.

The purpose of this brief is not to argue that ComEd default service is always better, or that retail supply is always worse. It is to explain how the system works so Illinois ratepayers can compare options with a better benchmark.

02

Analysis Fundamentals

What is measured

In northern Illinois, ComEd is the regulated delivery utility. ComEd owns and operates the local distribution system that brings electricity to homes and businesses. If a storm knocks out service, a meter issue occurs, or local wires need repair, the customer still contacts ComEd. Choosing a third-party supplier does not change the physical delivery path.

The supply side is different. Illinois allows customers in the ComEd, Ameren Illinois, and MidAmerican service territories to choose who supplies the electricity supply portion of service: the public utility default supply option, or an Alternative Retail Electric Supplier, commonly called an ARES.

PJM sits upstream from the customer bill. It is the regional grid operator and wholesale market administrator for a large multistate region that includes the ComEd territory. PJM does not own the power plants or power lines it coordinates. It balances supply and demand, monitors reliability, and runs wholesale markets for energy, capacity, and ancillary services.

03

Bill Anatomy → Realized Supply Costs

Headline rate alone is not the benchmark. The published Price to Compare is not what a default-service customer actually pays: each month it is adjusted by the Purchased Electricity Adjustment, which can run either direction. The realized supply cost — the number worth comparing a retail quote against — is NET = PTC + PEA.

The table below sets the default-service supply lines against the ARES supply lines for the same month (May 2025), and states how each is treated in this analysis.

Line itemDefault serviceARES serviceModel treatment
ESC (“PEC”)Included in PTCReplaced by ARESCalculated in PTC
TSC (“PSC”)Included in PTCReplaced by ARESCalculated in PTC
PEAYesNoDefault adjustment
CFRAYesYesExcluded from NET

ESC = Electricity Supply Charge; PEC = Purchased Electricity Charge. TSC = Transmission Services Charge; PSC = PJM Services Charge. CFRA applies under both options, so it falls out of a like-for-like supply comparison.

Plotted across the full period, the two lines below track each other closely for most of the record — and then separate sharply. In May 2026 the adjustment alone added 8.166¢ on top of a 9.704¢ Price to Compare, producing a realized rate of 17.870¢. A customer comparing a retail quote against the headline number that month would have been comparing against the wrong figure by nearly half.

One scenario is held constant throughout this brief: a commercial account using 30,000 kWh per month, against a 7.8¢/kWh fixed ARES rate. Default-service cost is computed from that month's NET rate; ARES cost is the fixed rate applied to the same volume.

2024–2025$53,560.80$56,160.00($2,599.20)

Positive values are savings against default service; parentheses are additional cost.

Over the two years, the fixed contract in this example cost the customer $2,599.20. 2025 recovered much of what 2024 gave up, but not all of it. That result is the reason this brief argues for a benchmark rather than a verdict: the same contract is a good deal or a bad one depending entirely on the period it is measured over.

06

Sensitivity Analysis

The rate threshold is the useful benchmark. Rather than asking whether one particular quote was good, the more useful question is: what fixed rate would have exactly matched default service over the period? Across the 24 months of 2024–2025, that rate is 7.439¢. Below it a fixed contract came out ahead; above it, behind.

Note how steep the trade is. Half a cent on the contract rate moves the two-year outcome by exactly $3,600 on a 30,000 kWh account — which is why the comparison is worth doing carefully, and why a quote should be evaluated against the realized NET benchmark rather than against a headline rate.

The supply comparison in this brief focuses on NET = PTC + PEA for default service, while a retail supplier may charge energy, capacity, transmission, and contract-defined pass-throughs.

2026 Year to Date

Beyond the brief · EZ NRG

The scenario tables above stop at the end of 2025, where the source analysis ends. The rate record in Appendix A runs six months further. This section applies the same method, the same scenario, and the same fixed rate to those six months. It is an extension of the analysis, not part of the original brief.

PeriodDefault costARES costSavings / (Cost)
2026 YTD (6 months)$20,904.90$14,040.00$6,864.90
2024 – 2026 YTD (30 months)$74,465.70$70,200.00$4,265.70

Same scenario throughout: 30,000 kWh per month at a 7.8¢/kWh fixed rate.

Six months of 2026 returned $6,864.90 — more than double what the contract gave up across the two preceding years combined, and enough to turn the running 30-month result positive at $4,265.70. The break-even rate over the full thirty months rises to 8.274¢, from 7.439¢ over 2024–2025 alone.

None of this makes fixed contracts good or default service bad. It makes the same point the rest of the brief makes, with more recent data: the benchmark moves, so the quote has to be evaluated against the benchmark rather than against a memory of what electricity used to cost.

B

Sources & Method

All rate inputs in this brief come from a single source: ComEd published default-service rates for the 0–100 kW commercial delivery class, January 2024 through June 2026, reproduced in full in Appendix A. No figure in this document is drawn from any other data set.

The method is deliberately simple, so that any reader can reproduce it from the appendix:

QuantityHow it is computed
NETPTC + PEA for the month. The realized default supply rate.
PTCESC/PEC + TSC for the month, as published.
Default costNET × 30,000 kWh ÷ 100.
ARES costFixed contract rate × 30,000 kWh ÷ 100. $2,340.00/month at 7.8¢.
Savings / (Cost)Default cost − ARES cost. Positive means the contract was cheaper.
Break-even rateTotal default cost over the period ÷ total kWh over the period.

Assumptions and limits. Consumption is held flat at 30,000 kWh every month, which no real account does; a customer whose usage is weighted toward high-rate months would see a wider gap, and one weighted toward low-rate months a narrower one. CFRA is excluded from NET because it applies under both supply options and therefore falls out of a like-for-like comparison. Delivery charges, taxes, and riders are excluded for the same reason. The 7.8¢ contract is treated as an all-in fixed supply rate with no pass-throughs; real ARES contracts vary on exactly this point, which is why section 07 matters as much as the arithmetic. This is a supply-side comparison for one delivery class in one territory, not a bill audit and not advice on any specific contract.
